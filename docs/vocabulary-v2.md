# Vocabulary v2

These definitions govern the five-part edition. Each term states what
it means, what it excludes, a positive example, a contrast, and the
most common misclassification.

Coverage: every chapter in `docs/chapter-map.md` Parts I–IV has a
matching entry below, keyed to its chapter title. Part V (Design) is
deliberately excluded — its six chapters apply the vocabulary defined
here to specific genres and a capstone; they do not introduce new
terms of their own.

## Role

### Ground

- **Means:** the bass establishes a chord's root as the harmonic floor.
- **Does not mean:** any long or low note.
- **Positive:** C beneath a Cmaj7 voicing.
- **Contrast:** E beneath the same voicing creates Cmaj7/E.
- **Common error:** calling a sustained non-root a Ground merely because it sounds stable.

### Definer

- **Means:** the bass exposes chord quality through a characteristic tone such as the third or seventh.
- **Does not mean:** every non-root bass note.
- **Positive:** E beneath an upper C–G–B structure makes major quality explicit.
- **Contrast:** G beneath C major changes inversion without defining major versus minor.
- **Common error:** confusing definition with inversion.

### Inverter

- **Means:** a chord tone other than the root becomes the lowest pitch and reorganizes the sonority.
- **Does not mean:** an unrelated chromatic bass note.
- **Positive:** E beneath C major produces C/E.
- **Contrast:** A beneath C–E–G reframes the notes as Am7.
- **Common error:** calling every slash chord an inversion even when the bass is not a chord tone.

### Pedal

- **Means:** one sustained or repeated bass pitch persists while upper harmony changes.
- **Does not mean:** a long bass note under one unchanging chord.
- **Positive:** C beneath Cmaj7, Fmaj7/C, and G7/C.
- **Contrast:** C held beneath Cmaj7 alone is duration, not yet a pedal relationship.
- **Common error:** naming the note in isolation instead of checking for harmonic change above it.

### Reframer

- **Means:** the bass gives an upper structure a different harmonic interpretation.
- **Does not mean:** harmless register "colour."
- **Positive:** A beneath C–E–G produces Am7.
- **Contrast:** E beneath C–E–G produces a straightforward first inversion.
- **Common error:** using "colour" when the chord identity has audibly changed.

### Driver

- **Means:** the bass's primary responsibility is propulsion through repeated attacks, articulation, register, or directional insistence.
- **Does not mean:** simply playing many notes.
- **Positive:** a recurring eighth-note dominant-funk cell that carries momentum.
- **Contrast:** equally dense answering phrases may be conversational rather than driving.
- **Common error:** equating density with function.

### Supporter

- **Means:** the bass deliberately simplifies its material to stabilize active harmony or another busy voice.
- **Does not mean:** unimaginative root playing.
- **Positive:** predictable roots and fifths beneath syncopated upper chords.
- **Contrast:** the same notes used as an insistent ostinato may function as a Driver.
- **Common error:** judging the line without hearing the texture it supports.

### Conversationalist

- **Means:** the bass participates in a perceivable exchange with another voice by shadowing, answering, contradicting, or leaving room.
- **Does not mean:** any independent fill.
- **Positive:** right-hand call followed by a bass response in the open half-bar.
- **Contrast:** a fill with no audible prompt is ornament or motion, not conversation.
- **Common error:** identifying dialogue from the bass staff alone.

## Motion

### Staying

- **Means:** the bass deliberately does not travel across a span, functioning as a motion *choice* (the zero case), not an absence of one.
- **Does not mean:** a Pedal. Staying requires no harmonic change above it — a root held through two bars of the same unchanging chord is Staying; the same root held while the chord above it changes is a Pedal.
- **Positive:** one root held for four bars of a single static Cm7 vamp.
- **Contrast:** the same root held while the chords above cycle through Cm7–Fm7–Bbmaj7 is a Pedal, not Staying.
- **Common error:** treating "not moving" as automatically meaning "Pedal" without checking whether the harmony above is actually changing.

### Stepping

- **Means:** a series of adjacent (second-interval) motions creates a smooth melodic contour.
- **Does not mean:** a route between two specific harmonic destinations, and not necessarily goal-directed at all.
- **Positive:** C–D–E–F moving stepwise within one prolonged harmony.
- **Contrast:** the same stepwise motion aimed specifically at landing on the next chord's root is Connecting Chords; Stepping only describes the contour, not the destination.
- **Common error:** calling any stepwise passage a "connection" — Stepping can wander within a single harmony with no destination at all.

### Approaching

- **Means:** a nearby note aims directly at a target from a half or whole step away.
- **Does not mean:** every note before an arrival.
- **Positive:** F-sharp leading to G.
- **Contrast:** D–E–F-sharp–G is a longer connection whose final F-sharp is the approach.
- **Common error:** labeling an entire connecting route as one approach note.

### Enclosing

- **Means:** upper and lower neighbours surround a target before it arrives.
- **Does not mean:** an arbitrary three-note turn.
- **Positive:** A-flat–F-sharp–G around target G.
- **Contrast:** F-sharp–G is a direct approach.
- **Common error:** ignoring whether both neighbour notes actually point to the same target.

### Arpeggiating

- **Means:** two or more chord tones of the *current* harmony become a directed melodic phrase, outlining the chord's own structure.
- **Does not mean:** any leap, and not a route between two different chords.
- **Positive:** C–E–G–C outlining a static Cmaj7.
- **Contrast:** a single leap from C to A within the same chord is Leaping unless it continues on to outline further chord tones.
- **Common error:** calling any single skip an arpeggio — arpeggiating requires a sequence, not one interval.

### Leaping

- **Means:** a large interval creates emphasis and requires recovery, whether or not the leap outlines a chord tone.
- **Does not mean:** an arpeggio. A leap to a color tone or an octave displacement is Leaping without being Arpeggiating.
- **Positive:** a low root leaping up an octave to punctuate a phrase, then stepping back down.
- **Contrast:** the same leap continuing on to outline two more chord tones becomes Arpeggiating.
- **Common error:** treating every large interval as "just an arpeggio" and missing the emphasis-then-recovery shape that makes Leaping its own device.

### Connecting Chords

- **Means:** a route links two structural harmonic destinations while preserving directional logic.
- **Does not mean:** any fill between chord symbols, and not necessarily entirely stepwise.
- **Positive:** C–B–B-flat–A joining Cmaj7 to Am7.
- **Contrast:** a self-contained lick that obscures the A arrival does not connect clearly.
- **Common error:** evaluating intermediate notes without hearing their destination.

### Contrary Motion

- **Means:** the bass and an upper voice travel in exactly opposite directions.
- **Does not mean:** Independence in general. Two voices can be independent while moving in parallel — Contrary Motion is the specific, stronger case of opposite direction.
- **Positive:** bass descending C–B–B-flat–A while the upper voice ascends E–F–G–A.
- **Contrast:** the same descending bass under an upper voice that also descends is parallel motion, not contrary.
- **Common error:** using "independent" and "contrary" interchangeably.

### Motion Maps

- **Means:** an entire phrase reduced to and read as a labeled sequence of this Part's motion decisions — a representation, not a new device.
- **Does not mean:** a new kind of bass technique. Motion Maps names and sequences the eight prior Motion terms; it introduces no new atomic material.
- **Positive:** labeling an eight-bar bass line bar by bar as Staying, Stepping, Approaching, Connecting Chords.
- **Contrast:** composing a new eight-bar line by ear without naming any of its motion decisions is not yet a Motion Map — the map is the act of naming the sequence.
- **Common error:** treating the map as prescriptive (a formula to fill in) rather than descriptive (an analysis of a line already chosen).

## Groove and performed feel

### Subdivision

- **Means:** a shared temporal grid (eighths, sixteenths, triplets) makes rhythmic locations countable at all.
- **Does not mean:** where on the grid a note begins — that is Attack Placement. Subdivision defines the grid; it does not yet place anything on it.
- **Positive:** declaring a groove's grid as sixteenth notes before describing any note's position within it.
- **Contrast:** describing a rhythm as feeling "like sixteenths" without stating the grid the notation actually uses.
- **Common error:** comparing two examples' syncopation or placement without confirming they share the same subdivision.

### Attack Placement

- **Means:** a note begins at a specific, written grid position — a notational fact.
- **Does not mean:** the Pocket. Attack Placement is what is on the page; Pocket is the performed relationship among pulse, subdivision, and ensemble that notation alone cannot capture.
- **Positive:** a note notated on the "and" of beat 2.
- **Contrast:** the same note performed a few milliseconds later than written is a Pocket/microtiming question, not a change in Attack Placement.
- **Common error:** calling a specific written attack placement "the pocket."

### Duration

- **Means:** the time between a note's attack and release.
- **Does not mean:** attack placement or touch.
- **Positive:** a quarter note shortened to an eighth plus rest while its onset stays on beat 1.
- **Contrast:** moving that note to the "and" changes placement, not only duration.
- **Common error:** discussing only beginnings when comparing grooves.

### Space

- **Means:** intentional bass silence changes what the continuing texture communicates.
- **Does not mean:** every unfilled subdivision.
- **Positive:** the bass omits beats 2 and 4 while upper chords continue and become audible answers.
- **Contrast:** silence after the whole ensemble stops is a boundary, not necessarily bass Space.
- **Common error:** claiming active space without an audible context or expectation.

### Syncopation

- **Means:** a weak-position attack, tie, or emphasis creates tension against a clear stronger metrical expectation.
- **Does not mean:** every offbeat or timing imperfection.
- **Positive:** an attack on the "and" of 1 tied across beat 2.
- **Contrast:** an isolated offbeat in an unmetered texture has no clear strong beat to contest.
- **Common error:** calling written syncopation "ahead of the beat."

### Anticipation

- **Means:** an upcoming structural note attacks before its formal chord or bar boundary.
- **Does not mean:** microscopic early performance timing.
- **Positive:** the Am7 root A enters on the "and" of 4 during the preceding Cmaj7 bar and ties across.
- **Contrast:** a chromatic F-sharp before G is an approach unless it already states the upcoming structural event.
- **Common error:** treating anticipation and approach as synonyms.

### Repeated Cells

- **Means:** a short pattern of recurring attacks, rests, durations, and contour establishes a groove's identity.
- **Does not mean:** any recurring pitch. The rhythmic/attack pattern itself must recur, not just a note.
- **Positive:** the same one-bar attack-rest-attack-attack rhythm repeated under changing chord tones.
- **Contrast:** a repeated pitch played with a different rhythm each time is not a Repeated Cell.
- **Common error:** identifying a cell from pitch repetition alone without checking whether the rhythmic pattern actually recurs.

### Variation Without Collapse

- **Means:** exactly one parameter of a Repeated Cell changes while enough of the cell remains for it to still read as the same identity.
- **Does not mean:** unlimited change. Change enough parameters at once and the result is a new cell, not a variation of the old one.
- **Positive:** the established cell's rhythm held constant while one pitch is displaced by an eighth.
- **Contrast:** changing the rhythm, the register, and the pitch content simultaneously produces a different cell, not a variation.
- **Common error:** calling any altered repetition a "variation" without checking how much of the original identity actually survives.

### Phrase Rhythm

- **Means:** groove behavior — density, placement, cell identity, variation — is organized deliberately across two-, four-, and eight-bar spans, not just within one bar.
- **Does not mean:** a single bar's groove. Phrase Rhythm requires observing a pattern across multiple bars.
- **Positive:** a groove that stays sparse for four bars, then thickens for the following four.
- **Contrast:** describing only bar 1's rhythm and generalizing it to the whole phrase skips the actual phrase-level decision.
- **Common error:** mistaking one representative bar for the shape of the entire phrase.

### Microtiming

- **Means:** small performed timing deviations within or around the notated grid.
- **Does not mean:** a written eighth-note displacement.
- **Positive:** consistently placing notated quarter notes a few milliseconds behind a drummer's pulse.
- **Contrast:** writing the note on the "and" is attack placement.
- **Common error:** using standard notation to claim a precise millisecond offset.

### Pocket

- **Means:** the stable, coordinated performed relationship among pulse, subdivision, articulation, microtiming, and ensemble response.
- **Does not mean:** a single note being early or late, or a notated syncopation.
- **Positive:** bass and drums sustain a relaxed but consistent relationship over a section.
- **Contrast:** one delayed attack is a local event and may be accidental.
- **Common error:** defining pocket as a dot's written position on the page.

### Performed Feel

- **Means:** the closing recognition that notated rhythm (Subdivision through Phrase Rhythm) and human microtiming/Pocket are related but not identical — a boundary chapter, not a new device.
- **Does not mean:** a new rhythmic technique. Performed Feel names the limit of what the preceding Groove chapters can claim from notation alone.
- **Positive:** stating explicitly that a written syncopation is a notational fact, while the pocket it's performed with is not verifiable from the page.
- **Contrast:** claiming a specific millisecond "feel" from notation alone collapses this boundary rather than naming it.
- **Common error:** using this chapter to introduce new performance instructions rather than to state notation's limits honestly.

## Boundary rule

Conventional notation can represent pitch, meter, subdivision, attack placement, duration, ties, rests, accents, articulation, and register. It cannot fully encode touch, physical attack, drummer interaction, stable ensemble pocket, or an exact human microtiming profile. Playback demonstrates the written claim; it is not proof of a human feel.

## Interaction

### Doubling

- **Means:** the bass reinforces a pitch or line actually stated in the upper voice (unison or octave).
- **Does not mean:** any moment where the two voices happen to share a pitch class. Doubling requires the bass to be tracking the upper voice's actual melodic content.
- **Positive:** the bass playing the same descending line as the right hand, an octave below.
- **Contrast:** a coincidental unison on one beat, with unrelated lines before and after, is not Doubling.
- **Common error:** calling a single shared note "doubling" without checking whether the bass is actually tracking the upper line.

### Independence

- **Means:** the bass maintains its own contour or rhythm, distinct from the upper voice, without being locked to it.
- **Does not mean:** Contrary Motion. Independence only requires not matching; the two lines may still occasionally move the same direction by coincidence.
- **Positive:** a syncopated bass rhythm under a smooth, unrelated right-hand line.
- **Contrast:** the same two lines deliberately moving in opposite directions throughout is Contrary Motion, a stronger, more specific relationship.
- **Common error:** treating "independent" and "contrary" as synonyms.

### Interlock

- **Means:** bass and upper voice alternate attacks so tightly that neither line alone carries the full rhythm — together they form one composite groove.
- **Does not mean:** Call and Response. Interlock's attacks interweave continuously into a single fused texture; they don't take turns with gaps between statements.
- **Positive:** bass and right-hand chords trading sixteenth-note attacks so the combined rhythm reads as one continuous line.
- **Contrast:** the same two voices alternating in complete, separated phrases with rests between them is Call and Response, not Interlock.
- **Common error:** calling any alternation between voices "interlock" when it is really a slower call-and-response exchange.

### Call and Response

- **Means:** one staff makes a complete statement, then the other answers it in the space the first vacated.
- **Does not mean:** Interlock. Call and Response requires a genuine handoff — a call, then a gap or cue, then a reply — not continuous interweaving.
- **Positive:** a right-hand chordal call in beats 1–2, followed by a bass reply in beats 3–4.
- **Contrast:** the bass answering while the right hand is still sounding is overlap, not response.
- **Common error:** labeling simultaneous activity in both voices as "response" when nothing has actually been vacated for an answer.

### Density Balance

- **Means:** the relative note-count and activity level between the bass and harmony layers is a deliberate, relational choice.
- **Does not mean:** how busy the bass is in isolation. Density is only meaningful measured against what the other layer is doing at the same moment.
- **Positive:** a busy bass line deliberately paired with sparse, sustained upper chords so the texture stays balanced.
- **Contrast:** the same busy bass line under equally busy upper chords may compete rather than balance.
- **Common error:** judging a bass line's density without checking the harmony layer's density at the same moment.

### Register and Separation

- **Means:** the vertical pitch-space gap between the bass and harmony layers controls clarity, weight, and muddiness.
- **Does not mean:** Density Balance. Two layers can have perfectly balanced note counts and still clash if they occupy overlapping registers — register is an independent variable from activity level.
- **Positive:** widening the gap between a low bass note and a close upper voicing to keep both audible.
- **Contrast:** the same voicing brought down into the bass's own register muddies the texture regardless of how few notes are involved.
- **Common error:** attributing a muddy texture to "too many notes" (a density problem) when the real cause is insufficient registral separation.

### Harmonic Rhythm

- **Means:** the rate at which the upper harmony changes chords constrains which bass strategies from every earlier Part are viable.
- **Does not mean:** Subdivision. Subdivision is the grid for note placement *within* a beat; Harmonic Rhythm is the rate of chord change, typically measured in beats or bars, and is an independent axis from it.
- **Positive:** a slow harmonic rhythm (one chord for eight bars) inviting Staying or a Pedal; a fast harmonic rhythm (a new chord every two beats) inviting Connecting Chords or Arpeggiating.
- **Contrast:** a dense, busy bass line (a Density Balance choice) can still sit under a very slow harmonic rhythm — note-attack rate and chord-change rate are independent variables.
- **Common error:** confusing "the harmony changes quickly" with "the bass plays a lot of notes" — closes Part IV by asking which of the preceding Interaction, Motion, and Role choices actually remain available once harmonic rhythm is fixed, which is also why it directly sets up Part V's genre-specific Design studies (a static funk vamp and a fast-moving jazz progression demand different answers to everything before this chapter).
