# Vocabulary v2

These definitions govern the five-part edition. Each term states what it means, what it excludes, a positive example, a contrast, and the most common misclassification.

## Role

### Ground

- **Means:** the bass establishes a chord’s root as the harmonic floor.
- **Does not mean:** any long or low note.
- **Positive:** C beneath a Cmaj7 voicing.
- **Contrast:** E beneath the same voicing creates Cmaj7/E.
- **Common error:** calling a sustained non-root a Ground merely because it sounds stable.

### Definer

- **Means:** the bass exposes chord quality through a characteristic tone such as the third or seventh.
- **Does not mean:** every non-root bass note.
- **Positive:** E beneath an upper C–G–B structure makes major quality explicit.
- **Contrast:** G beneath C major changes inversion without defining major versus minor.
- **Common error:** confusing definition with inversion.

### Inverter

- **Means:** a chord tone other than the root becomes the lowest pitch and reorganizes the sonority.
- **Does not mean:** an unrelated chromatic bass note.
- **Positive:** E beneath C major produces C/E.
- **Contrast:** A beneath C–E–G reframes the notes as Am7.
- **Common error:** calling every slash chord an inversion even when the bass is not a chord tone.

### Pedal

- **Means:** one sustained or repeated bass pitch persists while upper harmony changes.
- **Does not mean:** a long bass note under one unchanging chord.
- **Positive:** C beneath Cmaj7, Fmaj7/C, and G7/C.
- **Contrast:** C held beneath Cmaj7 alone is duration, not yet a pedal relationship.
- **Common error:** naming the note in isolation instead of checking for harmonic change above it.

### Reframer

- **Means:** the bass gives an upper structure a different harmonic interpretation.
- **Does not mean:** harmless register “colour.”
- **Positive:** A beneath C–E–G produces Am7.
- **Contrast:** E beneath C–E–G produces a straightforward first inversion.
- **Common error:** using “colour” when the chord identity has audibly changed.

### Driver

- **Means:** the bass’s primary responsibility is propulsion through repeated attacks, articulation, register, or directional insistence.
- **Does not mean:** simply playing many notes.
- **Positive:** a recurring eighth-note dominant-funk cell that carries momentum.
- **Contrast:** equally dense answering phrases may be conversational rather than driving.
- **Common error:** equating density with function.

### Supporter

- **Means:** the bass deliberately simplifies its material to stabilize active harmony or another busy voice.
- **Does not mean:** unimaginative root playing.
- **Positive:** predictable roots and fifths beneath syncopated upper chords.
- **Contrast:** the same notes used as an insistent ostinato may function as a Driver.
- **Common error:** judging the line without hearing the texture it supports.

### Conversationalist

- **Means:** the bass participates in a perceivable exchange with another voice by shadowing, answering, contradicting, or leaving room.
- **Does not mean:** any independent fill.
- **Positive:** right-hand call followed by a bass response in the open half-bar.
- **Contrast:** a fill with no audible prompt is ornament or motion, not conversation.
- **Common error:** identifying dialogue from the bass staff alone.

## Motion

### Approach

- **Means:** a nearby note aims directly at a target from a half or whole step away.
- **Does not mean:** every note before an arrival.
- **Positive:** F-sharp leading to G.
- **Contrast:** D–E–F-sharp–G is a longer connection whose final F-sharp is the approach.
- **Common error:** labeling an entire connecting route as one approach note.

### Enclosure

- **Means:** upper and lower neighbours surround a target before it arrives.
- **Does not mean:** an arbitrary three-note turn.
- **Positive:** A-flat–F-sharp–G around target G.
- **Contrast:** F-sharp–G is a direct approach.
- **Common error:** ignoring whether both neighbour notes actually point to the same target.

### Connection

- **Means:** a route links two structural harmonic destinations while preserving directional logic.
- **Does not mean:** any fill between chord symbols.
- **Positive:** C–B–B-flat–A joining Cmaj7 to Am7.
- **Contrast:** a self-contained lick that obscures the A arrival does not connect clearly.
- **Common error:** evaluating intermediate notes without hearing their destination.

## Groove and performed feel

### Syncopation

- **Means:** a weak-position attack, tie, or emphasis creates tension against a clear stronger metrical expectation.
- **Does not mean:** every offbeat or timing imperfection.
- **Positive:** an attack on the “and” of 1 tied across beat 2.
- **Contrast:** an isolated offbeat in an unmetered texture has no clear strong beat to contest.
- **Common error:** calling written syncopation “ahead of the beat.”

### Anticipation

- **Means:** an upcoming structural note attacks before its formal chord or bar boundary.
- **Does not mean:** microscopic early performance timing.
- **Positive:** the Am7 root A enters on the “and” of 4 during the preceding Cmaj7 bar and ties across.
- **Contrast:** a chromatic F-sharp before G is an approach unless it already states the upcoming structural event.
- **Common error:** treating anticipation and approach as synonyms.

### Duration

- **Means:** the time between a note’s attack and release.
- **Does not mean:** attack placement or touch.
- **Positive:** a quarter note shortened to an eighth plus rest while its onset stays on beat 1.
- **Contrast:** moving that note to the “and” changes placement, not only duration.
- **Common error:** discussing only beginnings when comparing grooves.

### Space

- **Means:** intentional bass silence changes what the continuing texture communicates.
- **Does not mean:** every unfilled subdivision.
- **Positive:** the bass omits beats 2 and 4 while upper chords continue and become audible answers.
- **Contrast:** silence after the whole ensemble stops is a boundary, not necessarily bass Space.
- **Common error:** claiming active space without an audible context or expectation.

### Microtiming

- **Means:** small performed timing deviations within or around the notated grid.
- **Does not mean:** a written eighth-note displacement.
- **Positive:** consistently placing notated quarter notes a few milliseconds behind a drummer’s pulse.
- **Contrast:** writing the note on the “and” is attack placement.
- **Common error:** using standard notation to claim a precise millisecond offset.

### Pocket

- **Means:** the stable, coordinated performed relationship among pulse, subdivision, articulation, microtiming, and ensemble response.
- **Does not mean:** a single note being early or late, or a notated syncopation.
- **Positive:** bass and drums sustain a relaxed but consistent relationship over a section.
- **Contrast:** one delayed attack is a local event and may be accidental.
- **Common error:** defining pocket as a dot’s written position on the page.

## Boundary rule

Conventional notation can represent pitch, meter, subdivision, attack placement, duration, ties, rests, accents, articulation, and register. It cannot fully encode touch, physical attack, drummer interaction, stable ensemble pocket, or an exact human microtiming profile. Playback demonstrates the written claim; it is not proof of a human feel.
