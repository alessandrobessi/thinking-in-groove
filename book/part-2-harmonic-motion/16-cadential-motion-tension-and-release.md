# Cadential Motion — Tension and Release

*Chapter 16 — Part 2, Harmonic Motion: How the Bass Moves the Music.*

**Term:** Cadential Motion

**Definition:** Bass movement that signals the end of a phrase through tension and release.

## Intuition

Some bass movement just changes harmony. Other bass movement announces, unmistakably, that a sentence is ending — that this is the punctuation, not another clause. That announcement is made almost entirely by the bass, regardless of what's happening in the other instruments.

## Mental Model

**Cadential Motion** is bass movement whose primary function is to signal the end of a phrase, through a buildup of tension followed by its release. It is less a single interval (like Root Motion) and more a role that certain Root Motion patterns take on specifically at phrase boundaries. The most familiar version — dominant to tonic, a descending fifth — is Root Motion's strongest pattern used at exactly the moment a phrase needs to close; what makes it Cadential Motion specifically is the position (phrase-ending) and function (closure), not just the interval.

Cadential Motion comes in degrees of finality. A full cadence resolves all the way home, closing the phrase completely. A half cadence resolves to the dominant rather than the tonic, creating a pause that still feels like punctuation — a comma, not a period. Recognizing which kind of Cadential Motion you're playing tells you, and your bandmates, how final this particular ending is supposed to feel, which matters enormously for how the next phrase should begin.

It's useful to hold Cadential Motion and Harmonic Rhythm in mind together: a cadence is often exactly the moment the Harmonic Rhythm slows down, giving the ear time to register that something is concluding rather than simply continuing.

## Visual Explanation

The Motion layer marks Cadential Motion with a double vertical tick placed at the point of resolution, in amber (`#D97706`) — a visual "period" or "comma" depending on whether the cadence is full or half, distinct from every other Motion symbol because it marks a moment in time (the arrival) rather than a path between two notes. The double tick typically appears at the end of a Root Motion or Deceptive Motion line, marking that this particular arrival is also a phrase boundary.

## Musical Example

Picture the final 4 measures of a 16-bar jazz-funk head in F, tempo around 108 bpm. Bars 13-14: harmony sits on Gm7 (ii), the bass playing a Driver-Role eighth-note pattern that gradually thins out, a subtle slowing of Harmonic Rhythm signaling an ending is near. Bar 15: root motion down a fifth to C7 (V), the dominant, held with rhythmic space around it — the tension point. Bar 16: root motion down a fifth again, resolving fully to F (I), landing exactly on the downbeat with nothing else happening rhythmically around it, letting the resolution breathe. This full cadence closes the head cleanly, distinct from an earlier half cadence at bar 8 that only reached C7 before continuing.

## Practice Ideas

- Play a ii-V-I and stop completely on the V for two full beats before resolving to I. Notice how the added space changes how "cadential" the resolution feels compared to resolving immediately.
- Practice ending a phrase with a half cadence (stopping on the V) versus a full cadence (resolving to I) and describe the difference in finality out loud.
- Listen to the last four bars of several tunes in a style you play and identify whether each ending is a full or half cadence, and how the bass's rhythm changes in the two bars leading up to it.
- Compose an 8-bar phrase that ends with a full cadence, then rewrite the same 8 bars to end with a half cadence instead, changing only the last measure.

## Summary

Cadential Motion is the bass declaring, through the shape and timing of its own movement, that a musical sentence is ending — and the degree of finality it chooses, full or half, tells the rest of the band exactly how much rest to take before the next one begins.
