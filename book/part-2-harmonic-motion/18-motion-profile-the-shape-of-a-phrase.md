# Motion Profile — The Shape of a Phrase

*Chapter 18 — Part 2, Harmonic Motion: How the Bass Moves the Music. Synthesis chapter: recombines this Part's eight prior terms; introduces no new vocabulary.*

**Term:** Motion Profile

**Definition:** The overall harmonic shape a bass line traces across a phrase (static, ascending, cyclical, wandering).

## Intuition

Zoom out from any single harmonic event — a root change, a pedal, an approach note — and look at an entire phrase at once. It has a shape: it might sit in place, climb steadily, circle back on itself, or wander without any obvious destination. That shape is built entirely from the individual devices covered in this Part, but it is itself something new: a property of the whole phrase, not of any single note within it.

## Mental Model

**Motion Profile** is the overall harmonic shape a bass line traces across a phrase — static, ascending, cyclical, or wandering — and it is the synthesis term for everything else in this Part. Every device covered so far (Root Motion, Pedal, Passing Motion, Approach Note, Substituted Root, Deceptive Motion, Cadential Motion, Harmonic Rhythm) is a decision made at a single moment. Motion Profile is what those decisions add up to once you step back far enough to see a whole phrase, section, or piece.

Four shapes are worth naming explicitly, because each has a distinct effect on a listener.

A **static** Motion Profile holds its harmonic ground — built from Pedals and a slow Harmonic Rhythm, with little Root Motion at all. It creates the sense of a fixed vantage point: harmony may shimmer or darken around the bass, but the bass itself isn't going anywhere.

An **ascending** (or descending) Motion Profile moves consistently in one direction across the phrase — built from a sequence of Root Motion events that keep climbing (or falling), sometimes reinforced by Approach Notes that lean the same direction each time. This shape creates a strong sense of trajectory: the listener can feel, before the phrase ends, that it's heading somewhere.

A **cyclical** Motion Profile returns to where it started, often via Cadential Motion at the phrase's end, having taken a specific and often repeatable route (frequently built on strong descending-fifth Root Motion, the backbone of cycle-of-fifths harmony). This is the shape of most functional harmonic forms: a satisfying loop rather than a straight line.

A **wandering** Motion Profile uses Substituted Roots, Deceptive Motion, and irregular Harmonic Rhythm to avoid a clear direction altogether — not aimless in the sense of careless, but deliberately unpredictable, refusing to let the listener anticipate where the next harmonic event will land.

None of these four are "better" than the others; they're different answers to the same question — what should this phrase feel like it's doing, as a whole? — and a full piece will typically use several different Motion Profiles across its sections, the same way a piece uses several different Role choices.

A quick way to identify a passage's Motion Profile without a page in front of you: hum only the roots of eight or sixteen bars, with no rhythm and no harmony above them, and ask where that reduced line ends up relative to where it started. If it's basically in the same place, you're hearing static or cyclical; if it's somewhere clearly different, you're hearing ascending, descending, or wandering — and comparing the route it took against the route you expected is often enough to tell the last two apart.

## Visual Explanation

Motion Profile doesn't get its own new symbol in the Motion layer — instead, it's read from the accumulated pattern of every other Motion-layer symbol across a full phrase, the way a weather map is read from many individual data points rather than one big arrow. A static profile shows mostly flat Pedal brackets and sparse tick marks. An ascending profile shows a run of Root Motion diagonals all sloping the same direction. A cyclical profile shows Root Motion diagonals that return, by the phrase's end, to where the first one began, usually closed off with a Cadential Motion double-tick. A wandering profile shows a visually irregular mix of dotted (Substituted Root) and bent (Deceptive Motion) lines with no consistent slope. Learning to "read the shape" of a fully annotated phrase at a glance — without tracing every individual event — is the payoff of having learned all eight prior terms.

## Musical Example

Picture a 16-measure fusion piece in A minor, tempo around 100 bpm, built to demonstrate all four Motion Profiles back to back, four bars each. Bars 1-4 (static): a Pedal on A under a slowly shifting upper harmony, Harmonic Rhythm nearly flat. Bars 5-8 (ascending): Root Motion climbs A, C, D, E in even two-bar steps, each arrival reinforced by a diatonic Approach Note. Bars 9-12 (cyclical): a descending-fifth cycle, E, A, D, G, closing with Cadential Motion back to a strong A in bar 12. Bars 13-16 (wandering): a Substituted Root reframes the harmony unexpectedly in bar 14, followed by Deceptive Motion in bar 15 that avoids the "obvious" resolution, closing the piece on a note that is stable but not the one a listener would have predicted four bars earlier.

## Annotated Notation

Across the full 16 bars, the annotated score's Motion layer would visibly change character every four measures: a static run of flat brackets, then a steadily rising staircase of diagonal lines, then a repeating descending pattern closed by a double-tick, then a scattered, irregular mix of dotted and bent lines — the shape of the piece would be legible from the Motion layer alone, before reading a single pitch.

## Practice Ideas

- Take a tune you know well and, listening only to the bass, sketch its Motion Profile shape across each section (verse, chorus, bridge) using the four categories: static, ascending/descending, cyclical, wandering. Do different sections use different shapes?
- Compose four 4-bar phrases over the same starting and ending chord, one in each Motion Profile, and notice how differently each one makes the identical harmonic "bookends" feel.
- Practice deliberately switching Motion Profile mid-piece — starting static, then breaking into an ascending run — and notice how that switch itself becomes a structural event, almost like a Role Shift but at the level of a whole phrase rather than a single note.
- Transcribe the bass line of a piece with a clear formal build (quiet verse, intense outro) and check whether the Motion Profile changes match the piece's overall intensity arc.

## Summary

Motion Profile is what all eight of this Part's individual devices add up to once a phrase is heard as a whole — a static, ascending, cyclical, or wandering shape that gives an entire passage its own sense of direction, built the same way a single note's Role or Motion is built, just at a larger scale.
