# Harmonic Rhythm — Who Decides When the Chord Changes

*Chapter 17 — Part 2, Harmonic Motion: How the Bass Moves the Music.*

**Term:** Harmonic Rhythm

**Definition:** The rate at which the bass line itself implies chord change, independent of what other instruments do.

## Intuition

The chart says the chord changes every two beats. Nobody's forcing the bass to agree. A bass line can move faster than the chart, slower than the chart, or exactly with it — and each choice changes how quickly the harmony seems to be unfolding, regardless of what the piano or guitar is comping.

## Mental Model

**Harmonic Rhythm** is the rate at which the bass line itself implies chord change, independent of what other instruments in the ensemble are doing. This is a subtle but important shift in perspective from the other Harmonic Motion concepts in this Part: Root Motion, Passing Motion, and the rest describe what happens at a single harmonic event; Harmonic Rhythm describes the pacing of those events across a whole passage — how often they occur, and whether the bass's own sense of pacing agrees with the written chart or deliberately departs from it.

A bass line can slow the felt Harmonic Rhythm below the chart's actual rate by using a Pedal under multiple written chord changes, making harmony that's technically moving feel static. Or it can speed the felt Harmonic Rhythm up, using Passing Motion and Substituted Roots to imply harmonic events between the written changes that aren't on the chart at all. Neither is more "correct" than the other; they're different decisions about how quickly you want the listener to feel the harmony unfolding, and the decision is entirely the bass's to make, moment to moment.

Harmonic Rhythm is also one of the clearest tools for shaping the arc of a whole piece rather than just a single phrase: a slow Harmonic Rhythm through a verse, followed by a faster one through a bridge, is a structural device as much as a harmonic one — it's one of the primary ways a bass line can make a piece feel like it's building, without changing tempo or dynamics at all.

Three diagnostic questions place a passage's Harmonic Rhythm relative to its written chart:

1. **Does the bass restate every written change, or skip some?** Skipping changes (via a Pedal or a long Anchor) slows the felt rhythm below the page.
2. **Does the bass insert changes the chart doesn't have?** Passing Motion and Substituted Roots between written chords speed the felt rhythm above the page.
3. **Does the felt rate stay constant across the passage, or shift?** A shift in Harmonic Rhythm partway through a phrase is itself a structural event — often coinciding with a Motion Profile (Chapter 18) change or a Role Shift (Chapter 9).

## Visual Explanation

The Groove-and-Motion boundary is where Harmonic Rhythm lives visually: the Motion layer marks each implied change with a small tick mark, in amber (`#D97706`), placed under the beat where the change occurs — not a line or bracket like the other Motion symbols, because Harmonic Rhythm isn't about a single event's shape, it's about the density and spacing of many tick marks across a passage. A passage with widely spaced ticks reads, visually, as slow Harmonic Rhythm; densely packed ticks read as fast — the diagram becomes a literal picture of pacing.

This makes Harmonic Rhythm the one Motion-layer symbol that's easiest to confuse with the Groove layer's Density (Chapter 24) marking beneath it — both are read as a rate of events rather than a single shape. The distinction is what's being counted: Density counts note-events regardless of harmonic meaning, while Harmonic Rhythm counts only events that imply a change of chord. A bass line can be extremely dense (many notes) while implying a very slow Harmonic Rhythm (all of them belonging to the same chord), and the two layers will show exactly that mismatch side by side.

## Practice Ideas

- Take a chart with chord changes every two beats and play a Pedal underneath it for a full chorus, restriking only occasionally. Then play the same chart with a new root on every single beat, adding passing tones between written changes. Compare how differently "fast" the harmony feels.
- Practice deliberately slowing your felt Harmonic Rhythm under a busy chart (via a Pedal) and then deliberately speeding it up under a static chart (via Passing Motion and Substituted Roots), and notice how much control this gives you over a section's energy without touching tempo.
- Map the Harmonic Rhythm of a tune's form (verse, chorus, bridge) by counting, roughly, how many implied harmonic events happen per section. Does the composer's structure line up with a change in pacing?
- Improvise over a static one-chord vamp and try to create the impression of faster Harmonic Rhythm using only Passing Motion and Substituted Roots, with no chord actually changing.

## Summary

Harmonic Rhythm is the bass's own decision about how quickly harmony should feel like it's unfolding, made independently of the written chart, and it is one of the most powerful tools available for shaping the pacing and structural arc of a piece without changing tempo, dynamics, or a single chord symbol.
