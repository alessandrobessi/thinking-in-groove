# The Approach Note — Arriving with Intention

*Chapter 13 — Part 2, Harmonic Motion: How the Bass Moves the Music.*

**Term:** Approach Note

**Definition:** A bass note (chromatic or diatonic) that leads into a target note from a half- or whole-step away.

## Intuition

Land on a note a half step early, on purpose, and let it resolve up or down into where you were always going. The destination hasn't changed. What changed is that the listener now hears the arrival as an arrival — as something aimed at — rather than as a note that simply appeared.

## Mental Model

An **Approach Note** is a single bass note, chromatic or diatonic, placed a half- or whole-step away from a target note, whose entire function is to lead into that target. Where Passing Motion connects two established harmonic points across a gap, often with more than one intermediate note, the Approach Note is concerned with exactly one note and exactly one purpose: making the very next note sound arrived-at rather than merely next.

Direction matters as much as distance. An approach from below (a half step under the target) reads as a lift, a small push upward into resolution. An approach from above reads as a settling, a gentle fall into place. Both are common; the choice changes the emotional color of the arrival even when the target itself is identical.

Chromatic approach notes — outside the key — are the more dramatic version, borrowing a half-step's worth of outside color specifically to intensify the pull toward the target. Diatonic approach notes — a whole step or a scale-step away, inside the key — are gentler, more like a soft anticipation than a chromatic shove. Both are Approach Notes; the difference is intensity, not category.

## Visual Explanation

The Motion layer marks an Approach Note with a short hook or arrow that curves directly into the target note, in amber (`#D97706`) — visually the most "aimed" of all the Motion shapes, distinct from Passing Motion's broader arched slur because it connects only two notes, not a run. Where the approach is chromatic, the visual language convention is to render the hook slightly thicker, signaling the extra intensity of stepping outside the key for just that one note.

## Musical Example

Picture a 4-measure neo-soul phrase in Eb, groove around 84 bpm. Bar 1: root Eb, functioning as an Anchor. Bar 2: a short Repetition Cell figure. Bar 3: on the "and" of beat 4, a chromatic approach note, D natural (a half step below the target), aimed directly at the Ab root arriving on beat 1 of bar 4 — the classic below-the-target chromatic approach, timed to land just late enough that its resolution coincides with the new chord's downbeat. Bar 4: Ab arrives exactly on time, and the chromatic tension from the approach note resolves the instant it's needed.

## Practice Ideas

- Take any bass line you already play and insert a chromatic approach note, from below, immediately before every downbeat root for one chorus. Notice how much more "aimed" the line feels even though the underlying harmony hasn't changed.
- Compare approaching the same target from above versus from below, back to back, and describe out loud the difference in feeling — a lift versus a settle.
- Practice placing the approach note in different rhythmic positions relative to the beat (right before the downbeat, earlier in the bar) and notice how its placement changes how urgent the resolution feels — an early preview of Pocket, in Part III.
- Transcribe a bebop or jazz-fusion bass line and circle every approach note you find. Are they mostly chromatic or diatonic? Mostly from above or below?

## Summary

An Approach Note borrows a single step of tension — chromatic or diatonic, from above or below — placed immediately before a target, so that arriving at the target reads as an intentional destination rather than an arbitrary next note.
