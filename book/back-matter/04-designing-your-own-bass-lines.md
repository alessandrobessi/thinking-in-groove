# Designing Your Own Bass Lines

*Back matter — Integrated Study and Appendices.*

Chapter 29 walked through one worked case study in detail. This appendix extracts that process into a plain, repeatable procedure you can run on any progression, plus two shorter sketches showing the procedure compressed to its essentials. Where Chapter 29 explained *why* each decision was made, this appendix is closer to a checklist: something to have open while you're actually composing.

## The procedure

1. **Write down the harmony and its harmonic rhythm.** Not just the chord symbols — how fast the chords actually change, and whether any of them last long enough to treat as one sustained harmonic idea rather than a sequence of separate ones.
2. **Choose a Motion Profile for the whole phrase before choosing any note.** Static? Ascending? Cyclical? Wandering, resolving late? Write the choice down in one word.
3. **Choose a Groove Signature for the whole phrase before choosing any rhythm.** Pick a Pocket (ahead, center, behind), a rough Density (sparse, moderate, dense), and a Repetition Cell length (how many bars before the rhythmic idea repeats or clearly varies).
4. **Mark the structurally important beats** — usually one per chord, sometimes two for a chord that lasts multiple bars — and assign each one a Role from Part I. Do this before deciding pitches; the Role constrains which chord tone makes sense, not the other way around.
5. **Choose the specific pitch for each marked beat**, using its assigned Role as the filter (an Anchor wants the root or fifth; a Definer wants the third or seventh; a Colorist wants an extension; and so on).
6. **Connect the marked beats with Motion**, choosing specific Harmonic Motion terms — Passing Motion, an Approach Note, a Substituted Root — for the notes in between, consistent with the Motion Profile chosen in step 2.
7. **Place every note in time**, using the Groove Signature from step 3 as the default and deliberately marking any note that departs from it (a Push, a Lay-back, a Syncopation Point, a deliberate Space).
8. **State the Groove Contract the opening bars establish**, and decide on purpose whether the rest of the phrase keeps it, varies it, or breaks it — and if it breaks, where the break gets resolved.
9. **Check every marked beat as a Layer Stack.** Read off its Role, its Motion, and its Groove together, and ask whether the three agree with the effect you intended. Adjust whichever layer is out of step.
10. **Play it.** Only now. Everything above was designed silently or at the very most sung; the instrument is where you find out whether the design actually feels the way it looks on paper, and it is normal — expected, even — to loop back to step 7 or step 5 once you hear it.

## Sketch A — a short, mostly-static vamp

**Situation:** one chord, Am7, four bars, moderate funk, meant to sit under a vocal.

- Motion Profile: static — deliberately, since the point is to stay out of the vocal's way harmonically.
- Groove Signature: dense, on-top-of-the-beat, one-bar Repetition Cell.
- Structural beats: bar 1 beat 1 (Anchor, root A), bar 3 beat 3 (Colorist, the ninth, B, briefly).
- Motion in between: Pedal throughout — the point of a static Motion Profile is that almost nothing here is Passing Motion or Approach Notes; the interest comes entirely from Groove, not from harmonic movement.
- Groove: the one-bar cell repeats in bars 1–2 exactly, gets a Variation Layer in bar 3 (the Colorist note is a Push, arriving an eighth note early), and returns to the plain cell in bar 4 — Groove Contract set in bar 1, varied in bar 3, restored in bar 4.

## Sketch B — a moving turnaround

**Situation:** a one-bar turnaround, Fmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7, played twice, medium-up jazz-funk.

- Motion Profile: cyclical — the whole point of a turnaround is that it returns to where it started, so the Motion Profile should trace a loop rather than a line.
- Groove Signature: moderate density, slightly behind the beat, two-bar Repetition Cell (since the turnaround itself is two bars long here, at two beats per chord).
- Structural beats: one per chord — F (Anchor), A (Connector, arriving by Passing Motion from F), D (Definer, the minor third of Dm7 given weight), G (Connector again, this time via a chromatic Approach Note from below, setting up Cadential Motion into the repeat).
- Motion in between: Passing Motion connects F to A; a short chromatic Approach Note connects D to G; the return from G back to F on the repeat is where Cadential Motion actually resolves, even though harmonically the "cadence" is really a turnaround loop rather than a final resolution.
- Groove: identical cell on both passes through the turnaround the first time; on the second pass, a Syncopation Point is introduced on the Dm7 beat, breaking the established Groove Contract right before the final Cadential Motion — a small, deliberate surprise timed to land exactly where the harmony is already doing the most work.

## Using this appendix

Photocopy the ten-step list, mentally or literally, before your next writing or transcribing session. The order is the important part — Motion Profile and Groove Signature before Role, Role before pitch, pitch and Motion before Groove placement, Groove Contract stated on purpose, Layer Stack checked last. Skipping the order and going straight to pitches is the single most common way this method collapses back into ordinary trial-and-error — which remains a perfectly valid way to write a bass line, just not the one this book is trying to teach.
