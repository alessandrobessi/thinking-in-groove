# One Groove, Many Roles

*Back matter — Integrated Study and Appendices.*

A single left-hand phrase, notated once, then reinterpreted under every Role term from Part I — the same notes, a different job each time, demonstrating that Role is a listening choice, not a property of the pitch itself.

## The base line

Here is the phrase, fixed for the whole appendix: the right hand holds a static Cmaj7, and the left hand plays a short, recurring figure built from five pitches — **C, E, G, B, D** — in a dotted-quarter/eighth/quarter rhythm landing its first note on beat 1, its second on the "and" of beat 2, and its third on beat 4, cycling through those five pitches across eight bars. Nothing about the left hand's pitches or rhythm changes across the eight reinterpretations below. Only the listening frame changes — and, in performance, only a handful of small physical decisions (attack, dynamic, which note gets emphasized) that a player makes *because* of which Role they've chosen. Two reinterpretations (Pedal and Reframer) ask you to imagine the right hand's harmony changing too, exactly the way the book's own Chapter 4 and Chapter 5 require an upper structure to actually move or hold fixed independently of the bass — noted explicitly where that happens.

This is the central claim of Part I stated as directly as this book can state it: identical notes, identical rhythm, eight different jobs.

## Reinterpretation 1 — Ground

Play the line flat, evenly, every note the same dynamic, C given the slightest extra weight each time it recurs. Nothing pulls anywhere. The ear parks on this line as the stable floor under the harmony and stops paying close attention to it — which is exactly what a **Ground** is for.

## Reinterpretation 2 — Definer

Now bring out the B on beat 4 — not louder in a crude sense, but held a fraction longer, given room. B is the major seventh of Cmaj7; the instant it's foregrounded, the line stops sounding like "the bass" and starts sounding like it's specifically telling you this chord is a *major seven*, not some other quality of C. Same notes. New job: a **Definer**.

## Reinterpretation 3 — Inverter

Bring out E instead of B, but with a different intention than Reinterpretation 2: don't think of it as revealing chord quality, think of it as briefly becoming the harmony's actual floor. For that instant, the sonority reorganizes into C/E — first inversion, less settled than the plain root position, its balance leaning forward rather than resting. The identical note, B, would do something even stronger — heard this way, it reframes the whole chord as Cmaj7/B, an unstable sonority that pulls toward stepwise resolution. The same pitch that was a Definer in Reinterpretation 2 becomes an **Inverter** the moment your intention shifts from "which quality is this" to "which pitch is the floor right now."

## Reinterpretation 4 — Pedal

Now break the appendix's own premise on purpose: imagine the right hand moving from Cmaj7 to Fmaj7 to G7 across the phrase while the left hand keeps recurring around C exactly as written, never following the harmony anywhere. C stops sounding like a settled root and starts sounding like a fixed point the harmony is measured against — confirmed while Fmaj7 stays consonant with it, increasingly charged once G7 makes it a genuine dissonance the ear expects to resolve. The decision isn't "hold a long note." It's "refuse to move while the harmony moves." That's a **Pedal**.

## Reinterpretation 5 — Reframer

Break the premise a second way: imagine the right hand holds a fixed E-G-B shape — not Cmaj7, just that one triad, unmoving — and the left hand's chosen pitch decides what the combined harmony actually is. Landing on C, as written, completes Cmaj7. But if this phrase's target note were A instead, the identical E-G-B above it would spell Am7 — the same fixed upper shape, an entirely different chord, purely because of what's supplied from below. The bass isn't adding color to a chord that's already decided; it's deciding which chord the upper notes belong to. That's a **Reframer**.

## Reinterpretation 6 — Driver

Add a hair more attack to every note, and think of the dotted-quarter/eighth/quarter rhythm as a repeating rhythmic engine rather than as a melodic shape. Play it slightly louder than Reinterpretation 1, with more separation between notes. The harmony becomes almost incidental; what the listener tracks is the motor, and if the figure stopped repeating, the music would lose its forward pressure entirely. That's a **Driver**.

## Reinterpretation 7 — Supporter

Now imagine the right hand is busy — a florid, active melodic line filling every beat — and play the identical left-hand figure as plainly and unobtrusively as you can manage, contributing exactly the harmonic weight the texture needs and nothing more. The simplicity is deliberate, not a lack of ideas: the bass is declining to compete for attention it could easily take. Ask, while you play it, whether the whole texture sounds clearer for the bass staying out of the way. That's a **Supporter**.

## Reinterpretation 8 — Conversationalist

Now play the line with your attention entirely on an imagined drum or comping figure happening around it, timing each of the three attacks to answer something that figure just did rather than to serve the harmony at all. The harmonic content (C-E-G-B-D over Cmaj7) is unchanged, but the reason each note exists has shifted entirely outside the chord, into a relationship with another voice. That's a **Conversationalist**.

## What this demonstrates

Nothing about the pitch content changed across these eight reinterpretations — and in two cases (Pedal, Reframer), what changed wasn't even a performance nuance but which independent harmonic context you imagined surrounding the identical phrase. In every case, the reason each note existed shifted because a particular Role had been chosen first. This is the practical proof behind this book's Core Promise: the question "which note" is frequently the wrong question to start with. "Which job" comes first, and the notes that follow are frequently negotiable once the job is fixed.
