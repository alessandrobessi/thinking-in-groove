# One Groove, Many Roles

*Back matter — Integrated Study and Appendices.*

A single 8-bar bass line, notated once, then reinterpreted under every Role term from Part I — the same notes, a different job each time, demonstrating that Role is a listening choice, not a property of the pitch itself.

## The base line

Here is the line, fixed for the whole appendix: eight bars in C, over a static Cmaj7 — the arrival chord of the book's own Dm7-G7-Cmaj7 laboratory, held here rather than moving on, precisely so this appendix's claim can be tested without the confound of the harmony changing underneath it — using only five pitches — **C, E, G, B, D** — in this rhythm, repeated with small variation across the phrase: a dotted-quarter/eighth/quarter shape landing the first note on beat 1, the second on the "and" of beat 2, and the third on beat 4. Nothing about the pitches or the rhythm changes across the eight reinterpretations below. Only the listening frame changes — and, in performance, only a handful of small physical decisions (attack, dynamic, which beat gets emphasized) that a player makes *because* of which Role they've chosen.

This is the central claim of Part I stated as directly as this book can state it: identical notes, identical rhythm, eight different jobs.

## Reinterpretation 1 — Anchor

Play the line flat, evenly, every note the same dynamic, C given the slightest extra weight each time it recurs. Nothing pulls anywhere. The ear parks on this line as the stable floor under the harmony and stops paying close attention to it — which is exactly what an **Anchor** is for.

## Reinterpretation 2 — Definer

Now bring out the B on beat 4 — not louder in a crude sense, but held a fraction longer, given room. B is the major seventh of Cmaj7; the instant it's foregrounded, the line stops sounding like "the bass" and starts sounding like it's specifically telling you this chord is a *major seven*, not some other quality of C. Same notes. New job: a **Definer**.

## Reinterpretation 3 — Connector

Imagine this line sitting between two different chords rather than static Cmaj7 throughout — say, arriving from an Am7 in the bar before and leading toward an Fmaj7 in the bar after. Play the same five pitches, but lean very slightly forward into the D at the end of the phrase, as if it's reaching toward something. The line stops sounding like a destination and starts sounding like a bridge — a **Connector**.

## Reinterpretation 4 — Driver

Add a hair more attack to every note, and think of the dotted-quarter/eighth/quarter rhythm as a repeating rhythmic engine rather than as a melodic shape. Play it slightly louder than reinterpretation 1, with more separation between notes. The harmony becomes almost incidental; what the listener tracks is the motor. That's a **Driver**.

## Reinterpretation 5 — Colorist

Bring out the D instead of the B — the 9th of Cmaj7 rather than the root or the 7th — by placing it a touch higher in dynamic and letting it ring slightly longer than its written value. The line now reads as reaching for a specific flavor of brightness rather than for structural clarity. That's a **Colorist**.

## Reinterpretation 6 — Shadow

Imagine a keyboard or horn playing this exact five-note, three-attack shape a register above, note for note. Play the bass line in unison with it, matching its phrasing and dynamics as closely as possible rather than asserting an independent character. The bass disappears into doubling — a **Shadow**.

## Reinterpretation 7 — Voice-Leader

Instead of thinking of C-E-G-B-D as a fixed shape, think of each note as chosen because it sits a smooth step or third from wherever the previous phrase's bass note left off — the specific pitches matter less here than the fact that each one was chosen for how it connects to its neighbor, not for its function against the chord. Play the same five notes, but mentally frame each one as an arrival from the note before it, not as an independent statement. This is a subtler shift than the others, but it changes how you attack each note: less separated, more connected, almost slurred in feel. That's a **Voice-Leader**.

## Reinterpretation 8 — Commentator

Now play the line with your attention entirely on an imagined drum or comping figure happening around it, timing each of the three attacks to answer something that figure just did rather than to serve the harmony at all. The harmonic content (C-E-G-B-D over Cmaj7) is unchanged, but the reason each note exists has shifted entirely outside the chord. That's a **Commentator**.

## What this demonstrates

Nothing about the pitch content changed across these eight reinterpretations. What changed, every time, was a small, specific performance decision — dynamic emphasis, attack, timing relative to an imagined context — made *because* a particular Role had been chosen first. This is the practical proof behind this book's Core Promise: the question "which note" is frequently the wrong question to start with. "Which job" comes first, and the notes that follow are frequently negotiable once the job is fixed.
