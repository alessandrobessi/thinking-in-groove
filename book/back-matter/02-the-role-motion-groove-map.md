# The Role / Motion / Groove / Interaction Map

*Back matter — Integrated Study and Appendices.*

A complete quick-reference chart of every vocabulary term in this book, grouped by Part, each with its one-line definition (copied verbatim from `docs/vocabulary-v2.md`) and chapter number. Use this page the way you'd use a harmonic cheat-sheet: not to learn a term for the first time, but to look one up fast while transcribing a line you're studying or composing one of your own.

## How to use this page

While transcribing: for each bass note or short phrase you're studying, ask the questions this book keeps returning to — what job is it doing (Role), how is it moving the harmony (Motion), how does it sit in time (Groove), how does it relate to the layer above it (Interaction) — and use the tables below to find the exact term that names your answer. If nothing in a table quite fits, the closest term is usually good enough; this vocabulary is meant to compress judgment, not to replace it.

While composing: work top-down, the way Chapter 35's Design Algorithm demonstrates — fix the harmonic rhythm first, then assign a Role to each structurally important note, then fill in Motion between them, then Groove in time, then Interaction with the layer above. These tables are ordered to support that: skim Motion and Groove first for the phrase-level terms (Motion Maps, Phrase Rhythm), then use Role for the note-level decisions.

## A. Role — What Is the Bass Responsible For? (Chapters 1–8)

| Chapter | Term | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Ground** | The bass establishes a chord's root as the harmonic floor. |
| 2 | **Definer** | The bass exposes chord quality through a characteristic tone such as the third or seventh. |
| 3 | **Inverter** | A chord tone other than the root becomes the lowest pitch and reorganizes the sonority. |
| 4 | **Pedal** | One sustained or repeated bass pitch persists while upper harmony changes. |
| 5 | **Reframer** | The bass gives an upper structure a different harmonic interpretation. |
| 6 | **Driver** | The bass's primary responsibility is propulsion through repeated attacks, articulation, register, or directional insistence. |
| 7 | **Supporter** | The bass deliberately simplifies its material to stabilize active harmony or another busy voice. |
| 8 | **Conversationalist** | The bass participates in a perceivable exchange with another voice by shadowing, answering, contradicting, or leaving room. |

## B. Motion — How Does the Bass Travel? (Chapters 9–17)

| Chapter | Term | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | **Staying** | The bass deliberately does not travel across a span, functioning as a motion *choice* (the zero case), not an absence of one. |
| 10 | **Stepping** | A series of adjacent (second-interval) motions creates a smooth melodic contour. |
| 11 | **Approaching** | A nearby note aims directly at a target from a half or whole step away. |
| 12 | **Enclosing** | Upper and lower neighbours surround a target before it arrives. |
| 13 | **Arpeggiating** | Two or more chord tones of the *current* harmony become a directed melodic phrase, outlining the chord's own structure. |
| 14 | **Leaping** | A large interval creates emphasis and requires recovery, whether or not the leap outlines a chord tone. |
| 15 | **Connecting Chords** | A route links two structural harmonic destinations while preserving directional logic. |
| 16 | **Contrary Motion** | The bass and an upper voice travel in exactly opposite directions. |
| 17 | **Motion Maps** | An entire phrase reduced to and read as a labeled sequence of this Part's motion decisions — a representation, not a new device. |

## C. Groove — How Is Motion Organized in Time? (Chapters 18–27)

| Chapter | Term | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 18 | **Subdivision** | A shared temporal grid (eighths, sixteenths, triplets) makes rhythmic locations countable at all. |
| 19 | **Attack Placement** | A note begins at a specific, written grid position — a notational fact. |
| 20 | **Duration** | The time between a note's attack and release. |
| 21 | **Space** | Intentional bass silence changes what the continuing texture communicates. |
| 22 | **Syncopation** | A weak-position attack, tie, or emphasis creates tension against a clear stronger metrical expectation. |
| 23 | **Anticipation** | An upcoming structural note attacks before its formal chord or bar boundary. |
| 24 | **Repeated Cells** | A short pattern of recurring attacks, rests, durations, and contour establishes a groove's identity. |
| 25 | **Variation Without Collapse** | Exactly one parameter of a Repeated Cell changes while enough of the cell remains for it to still read as the same identity. |
| 26 | **Phrase Rhythm** | Groove behavior — density, placement, cell identity, variation — is organized deliberately across two-, four-, and eight-bar spans, not just within one bar. |
| 27 | **Performed Feel** | The closing recognition that notated rhythm (Subdivision through Phrase Rhythm) and human microtiming/Pocket are related but not identical — a boundary chapter, not a new device. |

Two supplementary terms recur across this Part without a chapter of their own, both bounded formally in Chapter 27:

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Microtiming** | Small performed timing deviations within or around the notated grid. |
| **Pocket** | The stable, coordinated performed relationship among pulse, subdivision, articulation, microtiming, and ensemble response. |

## D. Interaction — What Happens Between Bass and Harmony? (Chapters 28–34)

| Chapter | Term | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 28 | **Doubling** | The bass reinforces a pitch or line actually stated in the upper voice (unison or octave). |
| 29 | **Independence** | The bass maintains its own contour or rhythm, distinct from the upper voice, without being locked to it. |
| 30 | **Interlock** | Bass and upper voice alternate attacks so tightly that neither line alone carries the full rhythm — together they form one composite groove. |
| 31 | **Call and Response** | One staff makes a complete statement, then the other answers it in the space the first vacated. |
| 32 | **Density Balance** | The relative note-count and activity level between the bass and harmony layers is a deliberate, relational choice. |
| 33 | **Register and Separation** | The vertical pitch-space gap between the bass and harmony layers controls clarity, weight, and muddiness. |
| 34 | **Harmonic Rhythm** | The rate at which the upper harmony changes chords constrains which bass strategies from every earlier Part are viable. |

## Quick lookup by question

If you're not sure which category your question falls into, start here:

- "What is this note *for*?" → **Role** (A)
- "Where is this note *going*, harmonically?" → **Motion** (B)
- "*When*, exactly, does this note land?" → **Groove** (C)
- "How does this note relate to the layer above it right now?" → **Interaction** (D)
- "How do all of these choices come together in a finished piece?" → **Design** (E)
