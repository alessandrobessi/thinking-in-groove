# A Mental Model You Can Forget While Playing

*Back matter — Integrated Study and Appendices.*

There is a specific kind of failure this book has been trying to avoid since Chapter 1, and it's worth naming directly now that you've seen the whole vocabulary: a player who can perfectly recite what an **Inverter** is, who can point to a **Syncopation** on a page, who can explain in full sentences why a line is functioning as a **Supporter** — and who plays worse for knowing all of it, because every note now arrives with a small committee meeting attached. If that describes where you are right now, this appendix is for you, and so, in a sense, is the rest of your playing life.

## Naming is a scaffold, not a destination

Every term in this book exists to solve one problem: before you had the vocabulary, you could hear that two bass lines were doing something different, but you had no fast way to say what. "This one sounds more grounded, that one sounds like it's asking a question" is a real observation, but it's slow, and it doesn't generalize — it doesn't tell you how to reproduce the grounded feeling on purpose in a different key next week. Naming the difference — **Ground** versus **Inverter**, say — turns a vague impression into something you can deliberately choose, practice, and recognize elsewhere.

But a scaffold is for building something, not for living in permanently. Nobody wants a finished building with the scaffolding still up. The vocabulary's job was always to get you to a destination where you no longer need it named to use it — where you hear a harmonic situation and an Inverter simply occurs to you, the way a word occurs to you in your native language, without a grammar lesson running in the background first.

## The three stages this book expects you to pass through

**Stage one: conscious and slow.** You stop after a note or a phrase and name its Role, its Motion, its Groove, its Interaction with the layer above it, out loud, the way the Vary and Reflection sections throughout this book have been asking you to. This is necessary and it is supposed to feel effortful. Almost nobody skips this stage successfully — trying to skip straight to fluency without it tends to produce players who have absorbed a *style* by imitation without a mental model underneath it, which works until they hit a musical situation their imitation didn't cover.

**Stage two: conscious and fast.** The naming still happens, but it happens quickly enough that it no longer interrupts the time-feel. You can think "Ground, Staying, on the pocket" in the space of playing the note itself. This stage often feels like a plateau — you can name things instantly now, and it still doesn't feel like *music* yet, it feels like fast bookkeeping. That's an accurate description of what's actually happening, and it's a real, necessary stage, not a sign that something's wrong.

**Stage three: the vocabulary goes silent.** You stop naming anything. You hear a harmonic situation and a bass line arrives, already carrying a coherent set of Role, Motion, and Groove decisions, without the words for any of them appearing anywhere in your attention. If someone asks you afterward why you played what you played, you can still answer in this book's vocabulary — the knowledge hasn't disappeared, it's just no longer doing its work out loud. This is the state every chapter in this book has secretly been aiming at.

## Why this isn't a contradiction of everything else in the book

It can look like the goal of a book built entirely from named terms is to eventually stop using the terms — and that is exactly the goal, without contradiction. A term is only useful while it's doing work you can't yet do without it. Once you can hear an Enclosing figure coming and feel exactly what it wants to do to a phrase before you've consciously identified it as Enclosing, the label has finished its job for that particular skill. You don't need to actively discard it; it simply stops being the thing your attention is on, the same way a fluent reader stops consciously sounding out letters.

This is also why forgetting the vocabulary while playing is not the same as never having needed it. A player who never went through Stages one and two, and simply imitated recordings until something usable came out, may arrive at a similar-sounding Stage three from a different direction — but they typically cannot diagnose *why* a line of theirs isn't working when it stops working, because there's no named model underneath the instinct to interrogate. The vocabulary's lasting value isn't in the moment of performance. It's in every moment before and after performance — composing, practicing, troubleshooting, teaching — where you need to be able to take the instinct back apart.

## A practical test

Here is a way to check where you currently stand, honestly, on any given day: play something over a progression you know well, without thinking about this book at all. Afterward, try to describe what you just played in Role/Motion/Groove/Interaction terms. If you can describe it accurately after the fact, without having thought about it during, you were playing in Stage three for that phrase — the model was present in the result and absent from your attention while producing it, which is exactly the arrangement this whole book has been built to produce.

If you find you *can't* describe it afterward — if the phrase simply happened and you have no account of why it worked or didn't — that's useful information too. It usually means Stage one hasn't fully happened for that particular corner of your playing yet, and the honest next step is backward, not forward: pick the term that seems closest to what you just played, and go slow with it again, the way Chapter 1 asked you to.
